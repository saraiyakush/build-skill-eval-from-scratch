A product team was asked to add one small feature to a payments app: convert US dollars to Indian rupees. It looked like a single multiplication, the sort of change people expect to finish in a day and rarely bother to test properly. But in payments, customers expect every amount to be exact, so the team listed what had to work: basic amounts, fractional amounts, very large amounts, conversions in both directions, an unknown currency code, zero, negative values, and a failure of the exchange rate service. That came to 12 checks.
Testing those 12 checks by hand meant setting up a shared QA environment, installing the app, and typing values into forms, which took about three hours. Unit tests ran the same checks directly against the conversion logic in a fraction of a second.
Then the scope grew. Adding British pounds made three currencies, and three currency pairs to cover, so 12 checks became 36. QA still had three hours, so they ran the 12 most important checks and skipped the other two-thirds, usually the edge cases. The unit tests ran all 36 in under a second.
Adding four more currencies brought the total to seven. Seven currencies form 21 pairs, which means 252 checks. At about 15 minutes per check, testing by hand would take 63 hours, more than a week and a half of work for every release. The unit tests still finished in seconds. Later, when the team upgraded the service from Java 8 to Java 17, the same suite told them within seconds whether anything had broken.
The lesson is that "too small to test" describes a feature only on the day it is written. Every new currency multiplied the work of manual checking, while the cost of running unit tests barely changed. Tests let testers spend their time on real user journeys and let developers change code without fear.
